# The Mandate Gap

### What 739 European deals tell us about where advisory work is actually decided

**StrategyAI Research · Q3 2026 · Europe, Middle East and Africa**

---

## In brief

Between 1 June and 4 September 2026 we tracked 739 M&A transactions across EMEA and
recorded, where disclosed, every adviser named on them. That produced 1,472 named adviser
appointments across 768 firms.

Sixteen of those appointments went to a strategy firm. Just over one percent.

This is not because strategy firms did not work on these deals. It is because the work they
do is invisible in the transaction record. Banks and law firms are named in the
announcement because their mandate *is* the transaction. Strategy work is commissioned
before the announcement and delivered after it, and in neither window does anyone publish a
tombstone.

That gap is the whole problem of origination in this market. If you wait for the deal
record to tell you where the work is, you are reading a document that structurally cannot
contain the answer.

Worse, of those 1,472 appointments, the number named for post-close integration, separation
or operating model work is zero. Not a small share. None. Across 79 carve-outs, no adviser
in our corpus was publicly named for the separation itself.

This paper sets out what we observed in the quarter: where the deals were, what they were
worth, what shape they took, which consulting firms were named and for what, what companies
were hiring for and in which countries, and what that implies for Q4. It also sets out,
explicitly, the three things our data does not yet let us claim.

---

## Method, and what these numbers are not

Every figure here comes from our own corpus: 739 M&A transactions, 1,472 adviser
appointments, 1,130 role advertisements classified by intent, and roughly 88,000 tracked
signals. Deal values are as disclosed; 240 of the 739 transactions carried a public value.

Two honest constraints, stated up front because they change how the numbers should be read.

**These are not market volumes.** Our monthly deal counts move with our own ingestion, not
with the market. A chart showing deals rising in July and falling in August would describe
our collection schedule and nothing else. So this paper reports *composition* — sector mix,
deal structure, value distribution, adviser mix — which is robust to when a record entered
the system. Where we discuss direction, we say so and show the basis.

**Disclosure is not neutral.** Only 32% of transactions disclosed a value, and larger deals
disclose more often. Median deal size across the corpus is therefore a median of the deals
that chose to tell you, which skews high. We report medians by sector rather than a single
headline average for that reason.

## Finding 1 · The adviser record has a hole in exactly the shape of strategy work

Of 1,472 named adviser appointments:

| Discipline | Appointments | Share | Distinct firms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Financial adviser / bank | 867 | 58.9% | 475 |
| Legal | 499 | 33.9% | 252 |
| Accounting and transaction advisory | 59 | 4.0% | 14 |
| Communications and IR | 31 | 2.1% | 27 |
| **Strategy** | **16** | **1.1%** | **11** |

A communications firm is more likely to be named on a European deal than a strategy firm.

The league table underneath is what you would expect. Goldman Sachs and J.P. Morgan tie at
20 appointments each, Barclays 19, Citi 17, then Morgan Stanley, Rothschild, Latham &
Watkins and A&O Shearman at 16. Nine of the top ten are banks or law firms. The exceptions
are PwC and Deloitte at 14 each, and both appear overwhelmingly in due diligence roles, not
strategy ones.

**Why this matters commercially.** Every business development function in consulting is
built on reading deal announcements. That source will tell you, accurately, who ran the
process. It will not tell you who is doing the separation design, the operating model work,
the synergy case or the first hundred days, because those mandates are not part of the
announcement. They are awarded on either side of it, usually without a press release.

The implication is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: **the deal record is a lagging
indicator of banking work and not an indicator of consulting work at all.** Origination
built on it is structurally late.

## Finding 2 · When a consulting firm is named, it is always before the deal and never after

The 1.1% headline understates the point. Look at *what* the named advisory appointments were
for, across all 1,472:

| Role type | Appointments |
|---|---:|
| Due diligence of any kind | 43 |
| Commercial or strategy advisory | 15 |
| **Integration, separation, operating model or first hundred days** | **0** |

Not a small number. Zero.

Across 739 transactions, including 79 carve-outs, not a single named appointment in our
corpus is for post-close integration or separation work. That work unquestionably happened.
Seventy-nine businesses did not separate themselves. It simply never appears in the record.

The pattern inside the consulting names confirms it. Every strategy house that does appear
is there for one thing:

| Firm | Appointments | What they were named for |
|---|---:|---|
| Boston Consulting Group | 3 | Commercial due diligence, commercial and strategy adviser |
| EY-Parthenon | 3 | Commercial and strategy diligence, M&A process lead |
| Kearney | 2 | Commercial due diligence |
| Bain & Company | 1 | Commercial due diligence |
| Oliver Wyman | 1 | Commercial due diligence |
| Monitor Deloitte | 1 | Commercial and strategic due diligence |
| Roland Berger | 1 | Commercial adviser |
| Strategy& | 1 | Commercial adviser |

Eight strategy brands, thirteen appointments, and with one exception every single one is
buy-side diligence before signing.

The Big Four sit in the same window and dominate it by volume:

| Firm group | Appointments | Dominant role |
|---|---:|---|
| Deloitte (incl. Monitor Deloitte, Deloitte Italy) | 16 | Financial, tax and IT due diligence |
| EY (incl. Ernst & Young, EY-Parthenon) | 16 | Financial and tax due diligence |
| PwC (incl. Strategy&, PwC South Africa) | 16 | Financial due diligence, transaction advisory |
| KPMG | 11 | Financial, legal and tax due diligence, reporting accountant |
| Alvarez & Marsal | 4 | Finance and tax, specialist adviser |
| Interpath | 3 | Financial adviser, financial due diligence |
| Kroll | 3 | Advisory, exclusive financial adviser |

**Two observations worth acting on.**

First, the diligence window is crowded and it is where every consulting brand competes for
the same visible mandate. Forty-three named diligence appointments against fifteen
commercial or strategy ones, in a market that also ran seventy-nine carve-outs.

Second, restructuring and turnaround specialists barely register. We recorded **no
appointments at all for AlixPartners** across the quarter. That is a statement about the
disclosure record rather than about the firm: this kind of work is engaged privately, often
under distress, and is precisely the category least likely to carry a public tombstone.

Carve-outs make the disclosure problem concrete. Of 82 carve-outs, only 31 named any adviser
at all. For 51 separations, the public record contains no adviser of any discipline.

## Finding 3 · The market is a barbell, and the middle is thinner than it looks

Across the 240 transactions with a disclosed value:

| Value band | Deals | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Under $10m | 33 | 13.8% |
| $10m to $50m | 35 | 14.6% |
| $50m to $100m | 21 | 8.8% |
| $100m to $500m | 66 | 27.6% |
| $500m to $1bn | 26 | 10.9% |
| $1bn to $10bn | 55 | 23.0% |
| $10bn and above | 3 | 1.3% |

Thirty-seven percent of disclosed transactions were under $100m. Twenty-four percent were
above $1bn. The $500m to $1bn band, the traditional heartland of mid-market sponsor
activity, held just 10.9%.

Two different markets are operating here and they need different coverage models. The sub
$100m band is high-frequency, low-disclosure, and dominated by serial acquirers doing
programmatic bolt-ons. The billion-plus band is low-frequency and heavily intermediated.
A single origination approach applied to both will be wrong for one of them.

## Finding 4 · Volume and value point at different sectors

| Sector | Deals | Share | Disclosed value | Median deal |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TMT | 146 | 19.8% | $48.3bn | $122m |
| Consumer & Retail | 108 | 14.6% | $23.9bn | $190m |
| Industrials | 106 | 14.3% | $53.0bn | **$677m** |
| Energy & Infrastructure | 85 | 11.5% | $31.4bn | $371m |
| Healthcare | 70 | 9.5% | $13.9bn | $275m |
| Business Services | 65 | 8.8% | $8.2bn | $218m |
| Financial Services | 59 | 8.0% | $46.1bn | $335m |
| Real Estate | 45 | 6.1% | $16.5bn | $188m |
| Materials & Chemicals | 36 | 4.9% | $6.3bn | $110m |
| Aerospace & Defence | 19 | 2.6% | $8.2bn | **$686m** |

TMT leads on count and trails badly on size. Its median transaction is $122m, the second
lowest in the table, and its volume is made up of small software and services bolt-ons.
A team covering TMT will be busy and will not necessarily be working on anything large.

Industrials inverts that. Fewer deals, but a median of $677m, and $53.0bn of disclosed
value from only 27 priced transactions. Aerospace and Defence is smaller again by count,
nineteen deals, with the highest median in the corpus at $686m.

Financial Services deserves separate attention: 59 deals, 8% of volume, but $46.1bn of
value. Fewer, larger, and more likely to carry regulatory and integration complexity that
runs for years.

**The practical read.** If a practice is measured on mandate count, TMT is where to look. If
it is measured on fee value, Industrials, Financial Services and Aerospace and Defence are
carrying the weight, and each of those has long post-close work attached.

## Finding 5 · One deal in nine is a carve-out, and carve-outs are the densest work there is

Deal structure across the 739:

| Structure | Deals | Share | Median value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Acquisition | 598 | 80.9% | $253m |
| **Carve-out** | **79** | **10.7%** | $206m |
| Minority stake | 44 | 6.0% | $74m |
| Merger | 10 | 1.4% | $1,010m |
| IPO | 6 | 0.8% | $2,400m |
| Joint venture | 2 | 0.3% | not disclosed |

Seventy-nine carve-outs in a quarter, at a median of $206m.

Carve-outs matter out of proportion to their share because they generate the most advisory
work per pound of transaction value. A straight acquisition transfers a company that
already knows how to run itself. A carve-out has to invent a company: separate the
financials, stand up the technology, split the contracts, decide what the transition
services agreement covers and for how long, and design an operating model that did not
exist before the deal.

That work begins before signing and continues twelve months past closing. It is also, by
the evidence in Finding 1, almost never captured in the announcement.

## Finding 6 · What companies advertised for while all this was happening

We classified 1,130 role advertisements from 508 employers between 29 April and 5 September
by the intent the role implies:

| Intent | Advertisements | Share | Employers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Strategy and transformation | 505 | 44.7% | 321 |
| Post-merger integration | 457 | 40.4% | 129 |
| Corporate development | 133 | 11.8% | 57 |
| Restructuring | 33 | 2.9% | 21 |
| Capital markets readiness | 2 | 0.2% | 2 |

The concentration is the story. Strategy and transformation roles are spread thinly across
321 employers, averaging 1.6 advertisements each. Post-merger integration is the opposite:
457 advertisements from only 129 employers, an average of 3.5 each.

A company posting one transformation role is doing housekeeping. A company posting three or
four integration roles inside a quarter is staffing something specific, and it is doing so
before the thing it is staffing for becomes public.

That concentration ratio, roughly two to one between PMI and general transformation
hiring, is the most useful behavioural marker in this dataset.

And by country:

| Italy | 52 | $42.4bn |

### What is being hired for, and where

Overlaying the 982 geolocated role advertisements on the same countries changes the picture
completely.

| Country | Deals | Role ads | Ads per deal | PMI share of ads | Transformation share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Germany | 136 | 375 | 2.76 | **79%** | 10% |
| United Kingdom | 157 | 227 | 1.45 | 19% | **70%** |
| Spain | 63 | 89 | 1.41 | 48% | 44% |
| Italy | 52 | 66 | 1.27 | 30% | 55% |
| France | 39 | 59 | 1.51 | 42% | 37% |
| Poland | 11 | 66 | **6.00** | 9% | 68% |
| South Africa | 15 | 53 | **3.53** | 4% | **83%** |

Three things fall out of this table.

**Germany is integrating. The UK is transforming.** Of Germany's 375 advertisements, 295 are
post-merger integration roles, 79% of everything it advertised and 65% of all PMI hiring in
the corpus. The UK inverts it: 70% transformation, 19% integration. Two of the largest deal
markets in Europe are staffing for entirely different work, and a practice covering both
with one proposition is mispitching one of them.

**Poland and South Africa are hiring far ahead of their deal count.** Poland shows six role
advertisements per transaction and South Africa three and a half, against a UK figure of
1.45. Neither is a deal story. Both are capability build-outs: transformation and delivery
functions being stood up in lower-cost locations, 68% and 83% transformation-weighted
respectively. That is nearshoring and global capability centre activity, and it is a
different mandate type to anything in the deal table above.

**Where the hottest specific roles are.** Concentrating on employers rather than
advertisements, Germany's 295 integration roles come from just 95 employers, an average of
3.1 integration hires each. That density is the single strongest behavioural marker in this
dataset. An employer posting three or more integration roles in a quarter is resourcing
something specific, and doing it before the thing becomes public.

### One caveat on this map

Our role-advertisement coverage is not evenly distributed. Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland
and Ireland together account for 87 transactions in the corpus and **zero** role
advertisements. That is a gap in our collection, not an absence of Nordic hiring, and the
Nordics should not be read from this table at all. We are extending coverage there and would
rather flag the hole than let a reader infer a market fact from it.

## Finding 8 · Serial acquirers buy on a rhythm, and the rhythm is measurable

Twelve buyers completed four or more transactions in the corpus. Their observed cadence:

| Buyer | Deals | Average gap | Days since last | Countries | Sectors |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apollo Global Management | 7 | 42 days | 31 | 4 | 6 |
| Blackstone | 7 | **20 days** | **118** | 4 | 6 |
| EQT | 6 | 57 days | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Bridgepoint | 4 | 80 days | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| Asker Healthcare | 4 | **21 days** | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| Mutares | 4 | 31 days | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Oakley Capital | 4 | 75 days | 18 | 4 | 2 |
| Bain Capital | 4 | 70 days | 32 | 3 | 3 |
| Verdane | 4 | 62 days | 52 | 2 | 2 |
| CVC Capital Partners | 4 | 69 days | 69 | 3 | 4 |
| Accenture | 4 | 58 days | 76 | 3 | 2 |
| Stonepeak | 4 | 64 days | 87 | 2 | 1 |

Asker Healthcare is the clearest programmatic acquirer in the set: four transactions across
four countries in a single sector, averaging 21 days apart. That is not opportunism. That is
a pipeline with a team behind it.

Blackstone, Accenture and Stonepeak are each now silent for longer than their own average
gap. We are not predicting a transaction from that. We are saying that a buyer with an
established rhythm that has gone quiet is a better-qualified conversation than a buyer
picked from a sector list.

## Finding 9 · How early the signal actually arrives

We publish lead times only where the earlier signal describes the same situation as the
announcement. Across seven verified cases this quarter:

| Company | Briefed | Announced | Days ahead |
|---|---|---|---:|
| ATG Entertainment | 21 May | 11 Aug | 82 |
| Gamma Communications | 9 Jul | 1 Sep | 54 |
| Delivery Hero | 2 Jun | 15 Jul | 43 |
| Commerzbank | 2 Jun | 8 Jul | 38 |
| easyJet | 31 May | 6 Jul | 36 |
| Bodycote | 28 Jul | 1 Sep | 35 |
| Pinewood Technologies | 27 Jul | 19 Aug | 23 |

Median 38 days. Mean 44. Longest 82.

Twelve further candidates were rejected during screening. In each case the earlier signal
turned out to describe a different event, a different company, or in one instance our own
output read back as an external source. Two examples of what did not survive: a takeover
matched to a job advertisement for an engineering apprentice, and a £3.8bn take-private
matched to a regional sales director vacancy. Neither is evidence of anything.

We mention the rejections because a lead-time statistic is only worth reading if you know
what was thrown away to produce it.

## What we cannot yet claim

Two things this dataset does not support, which we would rather state than have inferred.

**We cannot publish a hiring-to-deal lead time.** The behavioural thesis is that a company
staffing an integration function is telling you a transaction is coming. Testing that across
1,130 advertisements and 1,838 transactions produces exactly one clean match: TK Elevator
advertised a transformation role on 26 May and announced an acquisition 51 days later. One
observation is an anecdote. The obstacle is coverage, not method: our hiring corpus began on
29 April and spans 508 employers against a deal universe many times larger. We expect this
to become publishable, and we will not publish it until it is.

**We cannot read the Nordics from our hiring data.** Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland and
Ireland contributed 87 transactions and zero role advertisements. That is a collection gap.
Any regional hiring conclusion in this paper excludes them by necessity.

**Our headcount time series is not yet a time series.** We audited it while preparing this
paper and found the collector was rewriting cached values rather than observing change: 2,927
of 2,928 consecutive weekly comparisons were identical. It has been fixed, and refresh
cadence for companies with live signals has moved from 90 days to 14. But no genuine
week-over-week movement has been recorded yet, so nothing in this paper rests on it.

## What we expect in Q4

Four expectations, each tied to something in the data above rather than to sentiment.

**1 · Carve-out volume holds or rises, and the work lands late.**
Seventy-nine carve-outs at 10.7% of structure, combined with 457 integration advertisements
concentrated in 129 employers, describes a market mid-separation. Carve-out advisory demand
peaks six to twelve months after signing, which places the bulk of Q3's carve-outs into a
Q4 and Q1 delivery window.

**2 · Industrials and Aerospace & Defence carry the fee value.**
Medians of $677m and $686m, against a corpus median of $212m. Fewer conversations, larger
mandates, longer post-close tails.

**3 · The sub-$100m band keeps growing and stays invisible.**
Thirty-seven percent of disclosed deals and a disclosure rate well below the corpus average.
Serial acquirers at 20 to 30 day cadences operate almost entirely in this band. It will not
appear in league tables and it is where programmatic advisory relationships are actually
built.

**4 · Africa and the Middle East stay small in count and interesting in mix.**
Africa recorded 40 transactions at a $68m median; the Middle East 25 at $310m. Middle East
median deal size is above the European figure of $290m on a fraction of the volume, which is
a different kind of opportunity to a growth market and should be resourced as one.

## The conclusion we would draw

The single most useful number in this paper is 1.1%.

Strategy firms appear on roughly one in ninety named adviser appointments, not because they
do one in ninety of the deals, but because the announcement is the wrong document to look
for them in. The mandate that matters to a consulting partner is formed before the
transaction is public and delivered after it, in a window the deal record never covers.

Which means origination cannot be a reading exercise. It has to be a sequencing one: the
hire, the filing, the withdrawn bid, the buyback during an offer period, held together
against the behaviour that usually follows, and put in front of a partner while there is
still time to act.

Median 38 days, in our case. That is not a long time. It is the difference between a
conversation and a competitive process.
