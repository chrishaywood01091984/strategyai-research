# The Hidden Market for Consulting Work

### What 739 EMEA Transactions Reveal About How Advisory Mandates Are Really Formed

**StrategyAI Research | Q3 2026**

---

## Scope and Method

This paper draws on the StrategyAI corpus for the period 1 June to 4 September 2026. It
covers 739 M&A transactions across Europe, the Middle East and Africa, 1,472 named adviser
appointments across 768 firms, and 1,130 role advertisements classified by the type of work
they imply. Transaction values are as disclosed. Of the 739 transactions, 240 carried a
public value.

Two points of interpretation should be noted at the outset. Deal counts by month reflect the
pace at which records enter our corpus rather than underlying market activity, so this paper
reports composition rather than trend. Disclosure is also uneven: larger transactions
disclose values more often than smaller ones, which means medians are reported by sector
rather than as a single headline figure.

---

## 1. Why Consulting Demand Is Largely Invisible

Most consulting demand exists outside the public record. Banks and law firms are naturally
visible because their mandate is the transaction itself. Consulting mandates are different.
Commercial due diligence often begins before a deal is announced, while integration,
separation, operating model redesign, synergy capture and transformation work frequently
continue long after the transaction has closed. The consequence is that a large proportion
of consulting activity is never captured in deal announcements. This creates a structural
visibility problem for advisory firms relying on public records as their primary source of
business development intelligence.

The scale of that gap is measurable. Across 1,472 named adviser appointments in the corpus,
the distribution by discipline is as follows.

| Discipline | Appointments | Share | Distinct firms |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Financial adviser and banking | 867 | 58.9% | 475 |
| Legal | 499 | 33.9% | 252 |
| Accounting and transaction advisory | 59 | 4.0% | 14 |
| Communications and investor relations | 31 | 2.1% | 27 |
| Strategy | 16 | 1.1% | 11 |

Strategy firms account for slightly more than one percent of named appointments. A
communications firm is more likely to be named on an EMEA transaction than a strategy firm.
The issue is therefore not that strategy work is absent. It is that strategy work sits
outside the period in which announcements are made. In practice, the most valuable mandates
are often created before public awareness and delivered after media attention has moved on.

## 2. The Limitations of Deal-Led Origination

Many firms still organise origination around transactions. New deals appear on a database,
partners build target lists and outreach begins. The challenge is that by the time a
transaction is public, numerous advisory relationships already exist. Investment banks,
lawyers, diligence providers and management teams have often been engaged for months. This
does not mean transaction intelligence is unhelpful. It remains essential context. However,
it should be viewed as evidence of an event rather than evidence of future demand.

The league table of named advisers illustrates the point. The ten most frequently named
firms in the corpus are dominated by banking and legal practices.

| Firm | Appointments | Discipline |
|---|---:|---|
| Goldman Sachs | 20 | Banking |
| J.P. Morgan | 20 | Banking |
| Barclays | 19 | Banking |
| Citi | 17 | Banking |
| Latham & Watkins | 16 | Legal |
| Morgan Stanley | 16 | Banking |
| Rothschild & Co | 16 | Banking |
| A&O Shearman | 16 | Legal |
| PwC | 14 | Transaction advisory |
| Deloitte | 14 | Transaction advisory |

Deal-led origination also creates crowded behaviour. Multiple firms read the same
announcements at the same time and pursue the same opportunities. As markets become
increasingly efficient, visibility alone becomes less valuable. The real challenge is
identifying the conditions that precede major change rather than reacting to the change
after it becomes public.

## 3. Behavioural Signals versus Transaction Records

A transaction record tells us what happened. Behavioural signals tell us what an
organisation may be preparing to do. This distinction sits at the centre of modern
intelligence-led origination. Hiring programmes, leadership appointments, transformation
offices, PMI recruitment, cost programmes and capability build-outs all provide evidence of
organisational intent. They reveal activity before outcomes become visible.

Classifying 1,130 role advertisements from 508 employers by the work they imply produces the
following distribution.

| Intent | Advertisements | Share | Employers | Average per employer |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Strategy and transformation | 505 | 44.7% | 321 | 1.6 |
| Post-merger integration | 457 | 40.4% | 129 | 3.5 |
| Corporate development | 133 | 11.8% | 57 | 2.3 |
| Restructuring | 33 | 2.9% | 21 | 1.6 |
| Capital markets readiness | 2 | 0.2% | 2 | 1.0 |

The concentration is more informative than the volume. Transformation roles are distributed
thinly across 321 employers at an average of 1.6 advertisements each. Post-merger
integration is the opposite: 457 advertisements from only 129 employers, an average of 3.5
each. An organisation advertising a single transformation role may simply be maintaining a
function. An organisation advertising three or four integration roles within a quarter is
resourcing a specific programme.

Behavioural intelligence does not seek to predict transactions. Instead it attempts to
understand the underlying conditions that often precede strategic decisions. A company
recruiting multiple integration leaders, separation specialists and programme managers is
communicating something important even if no corporate announcement has yet been made.

## 4. What 739 Transactions Reveal

Analysis of the corpus produced several notable findings. Strategy firms accounted for only
a small proportion of named adviser appointments. Consulting firms that did appear were
overwhelmingly concentrated in diligence-related activity. And post-close integration and
separation work was absent from public disclosure altogether.

Examining what the named appointments were actually for makes the pattern clear.

| Type of work named | Appointments |
|---|---:|
| Due diligence of any kind | 43 |
| Commercial or strategy advisory | 15 |
| Integration, separation, operating model or first hundred days | 0 |

No appointment in the corpus was publicly named for post-close integration or separation
work, across a period that included 79 carve-out transactions. That work was evidently
performed. It simply does not appear in the record.

The behaviour of individual firms follows the same pattern. Every strategy house named in
the corpus appears in a pre-transaction capacity.

| Firm | Appointments | Nature of appointment |
|---|---:|---|
| Boston Consulting Group | 3 | Commercial due diligence and strategy advisory |
| EY-Parthenon | 3 | Commercial and strategy diligence, M&A process lead |
| Kearney | 2 | Commercial due diligence |
| Bain & Company | 1 | Commercial due diligence |
| Oliver Wyman | 1 | Commercial due diligence |
| Monitor Deloitte | 1 | Commercial and strategic due diligence |
| Roland Berger | 1 | Commercial advisory |
| Strategy& | 1 | Commercial advisory |

The Big Four occupy the same window and dominate it by volume, with Deloitte, EY and PwC
each recording sixteen appointments and KPMG eleven, predominantly in financial and tax due
diligence.

Perhaps the most important finding was the gap between visible activity and actual advisory
demand. The transaction record consistently captured the mechanics of execution but rarely
captured the broader change programmes surrounding those transactions. The implication is
clear: advisory demand extends well beyond the point at which a deal becomes public.

## 5. The Sectors Where Mandates Are Forming

Transaction volume and advisory opportunity are not always the same thing. TMT generated the
highest number of transactions, but many were relatively small acquisitions. Industrials,
Financial Services and Aerospace & Defence generated fewer transactions yet carried
significantly larger deal values and substantially greater execution complexity.

| Sector | Transactions | Share | Disclosed value | Median transaction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TMT | 146 | 19.8% | $48.3bn | $122m |
| Consumer & Retail | 108 | 14.6% | $23.9bn | $190m |
| Industrials | 106 | 14.3% | $53.0bn | $677m |
| Energy & Infrastructure | 85 | 11.5% | $31.4bn | $371m |
| Healthcare | 70 | 9.5% | $13.9bn | $275m |
| Business Services | 65 | 8.8% | $8.2bn | $218m |
| Financial Services | 59 | 8.0% | $46.1bn | $335m |
| Real Estate | 45 | 6.1% | $16.5bn | $188m |
| Materials & Chemicals | 36 | 4.9% | $6.3bn | $110m |
| Aerospace & Defence | 19 | 2.6% | $8.2bn | $686m |

TMT leads on transaction count with the second lowest median value in the corpus.
Industrials records fewer transactions at more than five times the median size. Aerospace &
Defence is smaller again by volume yet carries the highest median of any sector. Financial
Services accounts for eight percent of transactions and $46.1bn of disclosed value.

The distribution of transaction sizes reinforces the point.

| Value band | Transactions | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Under $10m | 33 | 13.8% |
| $10m to $50m | 35 | 14.6% |
| $50m to $100m | 21 | 8.8% |
| $100m to $500m | 66 | 27.6% |
| $500m to $1bn | 26 | 10.9% |
| $1bn to $10bn | 55 | 23.0% |
| $10bn and above | 3 | 1.3% |

Thirty-seven percent of disclosed transactions fell below $100m and twenty-four percent
above $1bn, with a comparatively thin band between them. These represent two distinct
markets requiring different coverage models.

For consulting firms this distinction matters. Origination strategies built purely around
transaction counts risk over-indexing on busy sectors while underestimating markets that
create larger and more valuable advisory opportunities. Complexity, integration requirements
and operational change often matter more than transaction volume alone.

## 6. The Role of Integration, Carve-Outs and Transformation

Carve-outs represented one of the most important themes within the dataset. Although they
accounted for a minority of transactions, they frequently generated extensive advisory
requirements. Separation planning, TSA design, operating model creation, technology
migration, organisational restructuring and synergy capture can run for months or years
after a transaction closes.

| Structure | Transactions | Share | Median value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Acquisition | 598 | 80.9% | $253m |
| Carve-out | 79 | 10.7% | $206m |
| Minority stake | 44 | 6.0% | $74m |
| Merger | 10 | 1.4% | $1,010m |
| IPO | 6 | 0.8% | $2,400m |
| Joint venture | 2 | 0.3% | Not disclosed |

Approximately one transaction in nine was a carve-out. Of the 82 carve-outs recorded across
the full corpus, only 31 named an adviser of any discipline. For the remaining 51, the
public record contains no adviser at all, despite separation being among the most
advisory-intensive activities in corporate transactions.

Transformation should therefore be viewed as the continuation of a transaction rather than a
separate event. The announcement may attract headlines, but the majority of consulting value
is often created through execution. Understanding where integration and transformation
activity is occurring provides a more accurate picture of future advisory demand.

## 7. Where Change Is Occurring

Transaction activity and organisational change do not follow the same geography. The
distribution of transactions by region and country is set out below.

| Europe | 674 | 91.2% | $242.4bn | $290m |
| Africa | 40 | 5.4% | $5.3bn | $68m |
| Middle East | 25 | 3.4% | $8.2bn | $310m |

| Country | Transactions | Disclosed value |
|---|---:|---:|
| United Kingdom | 157 | $65.8bn |
| Germany | 136 | $54.3bn |
| Spain | 63 | $5.7bn |
| Italy | 52 | $42.4bn |
| France | 39 | $7.7bn |
| Netherlands | 29 | $0.7bn |
| Sweden | 29 | $19.7bn |
| Norway | 20 | $7.0bn |
| Switzerland | 18 | $11.0bn |
| South Africa | 15 | $4.2bn |

The United Kingdom and Germany together account for forty percent of transactions. Italy
records 52 transactions but $42.4bn of disclosed value, reflecting a small number of very
large deals rather than market depth.

Overlaying hiring activity on the same geography produces a materially different picture.

| Country | Transactions | Role advertisements | Advertisements per transaction | Integration share | Transformation share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Germany | 136 | 375 | 2.76 | 79% | 10% |
| United Kingdom | 157 | 227 | 1.45 | 19% | 70% |
| Spain | 63 | 89 | 1.41 | 48% | 44% |
| Italy | 52 | 66 | 1.27 | 30% | 55% |
| France | 39 | 59 | 1.51 | 42% | 37% |
| Poland | 11 | 66 | 6.00 | 9% | 68% |
| South Africa | 15 | 53 | 3.53 | 4% | 83% |

Germany and the United Kingdom are staffing for different work. Of Germany's 375
advertisements, 295 are post-merger integration roles, representing seventy-nine percent of
its hiring activity and approximately sixty-five percent of all integration hiring in the
corpus. The United Kingdom inverts this profile, with seventy percent of advertisements in
transformation roles and nineteen percent in integration. Two of the largest transaction
markets in Europe are therefore preparing for substantially different mandate types.

Poland and South Africa record hiring activity well ahead of their transaction counts, at
six and three and a half advertisements per transaction respectively. Both are weighted
heavily toward transformation. This pattern is consistent with capability build-out and
nearshoring rather than transaction activity, and represents a distinct category of advisory
opportunity.

It should be noted that role advertisement coverage is not evenly distributed. Sweden,
Norway, Denmark, Finland and Ireland together account for 87 transactions in the corpus and
no recorded role advertisements. This reflects a limitation in collection rather than an
absence of Nordic hiring activity, and no regional hiring conclusion in this paper extends
to those markets.

## 8. How Consulting Opportunities Emerge Before They Become Visible

Mandates rarely appear suddenly. In most cases they emerge through a sequence of observable
organisational behaviours. Leadership changes may occur. Programme teams are formed. Hiring
activity increases. Specialist expertise is recruited. Strategic reviews begin. Only later
does a formal transaction, separation or transformation programme become publicly visible.

Two patterns in the corpus illustrate how such sequences can be observed.

The first concerns acquisition cadence. Twelve buyers completed four or more transactions
during the period, and the intervals between their transactions are consistent enough to be
described as a rhythm.

| Buyer | Transactions | Average interval | Days since last | Countries | Sectors |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apollo Global Management | 7 | 42 days | 31 | 4 | 6 |
| Blackstone | 7 | 20 days | 118 | 4 | 6 |
| EQT | 6 | 57 days | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Asker Healthcare | 4 | 21 days | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| Mutares | 4 | 31 days | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Bridgepoint | 4 | 80 days | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| Oakley Capital | 4 | 75 days | 18 | 4 | 2 |
| Bain Capital | 4 | 70 days | 32 | 3 | 3 |
| CVC Capital Partners | 4 | 69 days | 69 | 3 | 4 |
| Stonepeak | 4 | 64 days | 87 | 2 | 1 |

Asker Healthcare completed four transactions across four countries within a single sector at
an average interval of twenty-one days, a pattern consistent with a programmatic acquisition
strategy supported by a dedicated team. Several other acquirers have now been inactive for
longer than their own established interval. This is not a prediction of transaction activity.
It is an observation that an acquirer with a known rhythm that has become quiet represents a
better-qualified conversation than a name selected from a sector list.

The second pattern concerns the interval between a situation becoming observable and becoming
public. Across seven verified cases in the period, the difference was as follows.

| Company | Identified | Announced | Interval |
|---|---|---|---:|
| ATG Entertainment | 21 May | 11 August | 82 days |
| Gamma Communications | 9 July | 1 September | 54 days |
| Delivery Hero | 2 June | 15 July | 43 days |
| Commerzbank | 2 June | 8 July | 38 days |
| easyJet | 31 May | 6 July | 36 days |
| Bodycote | 28 July | 1 September | 35 days |
| Pinewood Technologies | 27 July | 19 August | 23 days |

The median interval was thirty-eight days. A further twelve candidate cases were examined
and set aside during review because the earlier signal described a different event or a
different organisation. Lead-time measurement is only meaningful where the method for
excluding false matches is applied consistently.

The organisations that understand these sequences gain a deeper understanding of where change
is occurring. Rather than pursuing opportunities after formal procurement begins, they are
able to understand context, develop hypotheses and build relationships earlier in the cycle.
The advantage is not prediction. The advantage is awareness.

## 9. Implications for Partners in 2027

The consulting market is becoming increasingly competitive, while information is becoming
more abundant and less differentiated. As a result, simply having access to transaction
announcements is no longer a source of competitive advantage. The next generation of
origination will increasingly resemble an intelligence capability.

Four implications follow from the evidence in this paper.

Integration and separation demand will continue to be created ahead of its visibility. A
market that recorded 79 carve-outs alongside 457 integration advertisements concentrated in
129 employers is a market in the middle of substantial execution activity, most of which will
be delivered in the periods following the transactions that created it.

Sector coverage should be weighted by complexity rather than volume. Industrials and
Aerospace & Defence recorded median transaction values of $677m and $686m respectively
against a corpus median of $212m, with correspondingly longer execution horizons.

The lower value segment will remain commercially significant and largely undocumented.
Transactions below $100m accounted for thirty-seven percent of disclosed activity and are
associated with the acquirers operating at the shortest intervals. This segment rarely
features in league tables and is where programmatic advisory relationships are most often
established.

Geographic strategy should distinguish between transaction markets and capability markets.
Germany and the United Kingdom are both major transaction markets preparing for different
work. Poland and South Africa show hiring activity substantially ahead of transaction
activity, indicating capability development rather than deal execution.

Partners will need to combine market expertise with behavioural analysis, organisational
understanding and signal detection. The goal is not to identify more deals. The goal is to
better understand where change is likely to happen and which opportunities are likely to
produce meaningful advisory demand. Firms that develop this capability are likely to have
earlier, better-informed and more commercially relevant conversations.

---

## Conclusion

The central conclusion of this research is that consulting demand is frequently invisible
within traditional market records. Transactions remain important, but they are only one part
of a much larger picture. The most significant opportunities are often created before public
announcements and delivered long after those announcements have disappeared from view.

The evidence supports this directly. Strategy firms accounted for 1.1% of named adviser
appointments. No appointment in the corpus was named for post-close integration or separation
work. Fifty-one of 82 carve-outs named no adviser at all. In each case the underlying work
plainly occurred, but the public record did not describe it.

Understanding the hidden market for consulting work requires a shift from transaction
monitoring to behavioural intelligence. The firms that make that shift will possess a more
complete understanding of where advisory demand is forming and how markets are evolving.

---

## Note on Scope

Figures in this paper are drawn from the StrategyAI corpus for 1 June to 4 September 2026 and
are traceable to source. Composition figures exclude debt and equity capital markets
issuance. Two limitations should be recorded. First, hiring activity cannot yet be connected
to individual transactions with sufficient frequency to support a published interval between
recruitment and announcement; testing across the corpus produced a single clean match, which
is insufficient for a general finding. Second, longitudinal headcount measurement is being
rebuilt following an audit conducted during the preparation of this paper, and no conclusion
here relies upon it.
